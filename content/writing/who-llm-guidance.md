Earlier this year, the World Health Organization (WHO) released comprehensive guidance on the ethics and governance of large multi-modal models (LMMs) in healthcare. They carefully note the need for ethics and robust governance, providing guidance to stakeholders on the basic ethical requirements that should direct decision-making in the development, deployment, and use of LMMs. The key aim of this article is to break down the key components of this framework so that you can talk knowledgeably about the central risks and key takeaways of this guidance.

## First – what is an LMM?

An LMM is essentially a multi-modal language model, so instead of being trained only using language, LMMs are capable of processing and producing a variety of inputs and outputs: text, images, audio, and video. They promise a richer and more comprehensive world view than LLMs.

The incorporation of LMMs in health care has the potential to revolutionise the way health care services are delivered and managed: assisting in managing complex cases, administrative tasks, treatment and drug development. However, as with all developing technologies, we’re presented with a double-edged sword: the promise of transforming patient care is met with a bevy of ethical concerns.

Confident deployment of LMMs in healthcare will require us to place ethical principles at the centre of appropriate governance. The following presents, firstly, the central ethical risks presented in this article, and secondly, what I take to be the central message in our approach to LMM governance.

## Risks

### 1. Veracity

One central worry surrounding LMMs, and LLMs more generally, is their veracity – the problem known colloquially as ‘hallucinations’. LMMs may present us with inaccurate, incomplete, or false responses. These false responses may be difficult to identify, given that current models fail to pinpoint the sources of their outputs. A general difficulty with such generative AI systems is that it is hard to identify the rules that the model learned – rules are merely patterns, observed in input data. The use of such models in health care should be predicated on a demonstration of their accuracy, and clarification of source.

### 2. Data

A second theme of challenges concerning LMMs has to do with data. Two concerns are relevant here: (1) legislation: we will need to determine whether LMMs comply with existing legal and regulatory frameworks – crucially, whether the training data was acquired in accordance with data protection principles; (2) bias: if the training data is biased, LMMs have the potential to replicate flaws encoded in the training data. Underrepresentation of perspectives and data from low-income and middle-income countries threatens to aggravate existing structural inequalities.

### 3. Accountability

Finally, there is a central need for accountability. If decision making, advice, or patient guidance is conferred to LMMs, then responsibilities at each stage of the development cycle will need to be set for those involved. Irrespective of the difficulties of assigning blame, there should be clear avenues for liability, such that victims of damage can claim compensation.

## How to approach ethics implementation?

While the framework did not emphasise the importance of taking a contextual approach to ethics implementation, it presents a central facet of their approach. We can consider this contextuality in three ways: the use of LMMs, the actors involved, and the lifecycle.

- **Use:** The framework presents ethical risks in association with potential applications or use-cases. This correctly highlights the sense in which considering the context in which an AI system is used is central to assessing the risks involved.
- **Actors:** Responsibilities should be defined contextually with respect to the actors too, including the developers, providers, deployers, and governments.
- **Lifecycle:** Risks, governance and accountability measures should be defined at each stage of the process, from design and development to deployment. During design and development, the focus is on the practices that developers can produce; in provision phases, the focus is on governmental measures to regulate and assess use of LMMs; and finally the deployment phase should involve all actors in the value chain. They highlight the central role of governments to determine whether a use should be permitted. Setting clear stages and defining the actors involved will be central in setting clear standards and expectations.

Central questions to ask are: Which actor is best placed to address relevant risks? And which risks should be addressed? How can these risks be addressed, and which ethical principles are relevant? And what roles should each actor have throughout the development cycle?
