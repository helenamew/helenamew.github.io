The [Global Health Conference](https://www.himssconference.com/en/homepage.html) (HIMSS24) was held on the 11th March. There, the Trustworthy and Responsible AI Network, or TRAIN, was announced. TRAIN is a network, founded by Microsoft, seeking to [‘operationalize responsible AI principles to improve the quality, safety and trustworthiness of AI in health’](https://news.microsoft.com/2024/03/11/new-consortium-of-healthcare-leaders-announces-formation-of-trustworthy-responsible-ai-network-train-making-safe-and-fair-ai-accessible-to-every-healthcare-organization/). Once we’ve run through TRAIN in more detail, we’ll be taking a deep dive into the various entities we might trust, or that practitioners seek to encourage trust in.

*How TRAIN seeks to improve the trustworthiness of AI:*

1. **Sharing best practices** – including both uses of AI in healthcare, and the skillsets needed to manage AI responsibly.
2. **Enabling registration of AI**
3. **Providing tools which enable practitioners to measure the outcomes of AI implementation** – including tools to study the efficiency, security and biases
4. **Facilitating the development of a federated national AI outcomes registry**

*What is trust?* In the context of AI systems, trust typically refers to the confidence that users have in the system’s ability to perform tasks accurately, reliably, and ethically.

*Why is trust important?* For a technology to be accepted and used by society, it must be trusted. But there is increasing evidence that people do not trust AI. Consumers are [wary about trusting AI systems](https://kpmg.com/xx/en/home/insights/2023/09/trust-in-artificial-intelligence.html), and only [6% of leaders welcome AI adoption within their organisations](https://en-gb.newsroom.workday.com/2024-01-10). Facilitating trust in AI has become an economical move – adoption and trust come hand in hand. How then can responsible AI practitioners build a climate of trust in AI?

## Trust in who?

We can approach the question of trust from many different perspectives; there are various entities that we might place trust in, and ‘we’ might be a practitioner, consumer, or CEO. Let’s take the context of healthcare, given TRAIN’s focus.

1. *Healthcare professionals’ trust in AI.* In the context of healthcare, we can think of the trust that **healthcare professionals have in tools**. Their trust should be understood as specific to particular use-cases – many individuals might trust the use of one tool in drug development yet be sceptical of another in diagnoses.
2. *Patients’ trust in AI.* We can also think of the consumer’s trust in AI. This might be trust directly in the outputs of a system, as when a user interacts with an administrative chatbot, and trusts that the opening times and contact details are accurate. But we can also think of a consumer’s trust in a healthcare professional using an artificial system – where a consumer will be more likely to trust the collaborative professional + AI duo if they are reassured that each healthcare professional has received rigorous training on the proper use of these technologies, and channels through which accountability is set.
3. *Patients’ trust in the company / body.* The consumer’s trust in a company might be bound up with their trust in a system. If a company demonstrates their drive towards responsible AI – providing ethics training, with a diversity of perspectives, and clear channels for feedback – then a consumer may be more likely to trust the systems that they deploy and use. On the other hand, mishaps and security concerns might facilitate distrust – which could end up undermining a broader societal trust in healthcare systems.
4. *Trust within healthcare systems.* We can also think of trust within companies. Do those higher up in a company trust the AI system enough to deploy it within their company? Do their employees trust that their bosses have carefully considered the implications of adopting these technologies for them? Do healthcare practitioners trust that they will be given sufficient training?

The question of trust should be considered relative to different entities and perspectives – creating trust within an organisation is very different from gaining trust in companies.

Just laying out the field in this way reveals that the task of building trustworthy and responsible AI is multifaceted and influenced by a variety of important factors. Trust comes in ebbs and flows – it can change over time, be lost easily, and is sometimes difficult to regain.

We should also keep in mind that trusting entities and others isn’t always a good thing. Practitioners who are seeking to bridge the trust gap should ensure that they are encouraging trust in genuinely trustworthy systems.
