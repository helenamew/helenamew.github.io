Before we dive into thinking about accountability, let’s get clear on what it is.

## Responsibility vs accountability

The first important point to understand is that accountability is not the same as responsibility. Responsibility is about who caused what to happen – I am responsible for some action A, when I make an informed, un-coerced decision to perform that action. If I decide to write a LinkedIn post, discussing my opinions on the recent EU AI Act, then I am responsible for any inspiration or offence that my post causes. My act – of posting – directly correlates with other people’s access to my opinions. And as long as we are aware of patterns of causation – of what caused what – responsibility is fairly easy to assign.

Responsibility and accountability might seem like two names for the same thing, but they’re not. While responsibility is about causation, accountability is about blame. I am accountable for a decision, if I am willing to take responsibility for my actions and face up to the corresponding blame, or praise. So, I am held accountable for my post, if I admit that my post was insensitive, and take the blame for any offence that I caused.

Ordinarily responsibility and accountability come hand in hand – my being responsible for some action A means that I am also accountable for A and the impact or effect of A. But when artificially intelligent agents are making the decision, responsibility and accountability come apart. Consider a generative AI chatbot, who – like me – posts an opinion piece on the EU AI Act. The chatbot is responsible for the post, and it’s being seen. But it cannot be held accountable: the opinions disseminated aren’t opinions ‘had’ by any entity, and blaming said system for offensive posts doesn’t sit right. We could respond to an offensive AI system by turning it off, or re-programming it, but without consciousness, or capabilities of feeling pain, blame doesn’t make sense.

This is called the ‘accountability gap’ – a gap between who or what caused an action, and who is to blame.

## Liability for generated content

Recent developments have made clear that seeming gaps in accountability are going to be filled. Efforts by companies to avoid accountability by claiming that ‘they didn’t do anything’ are not being received well.

Consider the airline company Air Canada, whose chatbot gave a customer inaccurate information which misled them into buying a full-price ticket, on the basis of false information concerning bereavement refunds – information which conflicted with the bereavement section of their website. Air Canada attempted to evade being held accountable for the advice given, because the bot was [“a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions”](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2024/feb/16/air-canada-chatbot-lawsuit).

To take another case, consider the chatbots used by car dealerships back in December, where customers were able to push chatbots into agreeing to a $58,000 discount on a new car, [lowering the price to a measly $1](https://venturebeat.com/ai/a-chevy-for-1-car-dealer-chatbots-show-perils-of-ai-for-customer-service/).

Cases like this convey two important messages. Firstly, that automated systems need proper oversight; secondly, that companies are liable for what their tech says and does.

## The AI Law

And recent developments in policy seem to be moving in this direction. On March 15th 2024, the same day that the EU parliament adopted the EU AI Act, Utah became the first state to enact a major AI statute, governing private sector AI usage. [S.B. 149](https://le.utah.gov/~2024/bills/static/SB0149.html) – the AI Law – pertains only to the use of AI to generate content, such as chatbot responses. It focuses on establishing liability for improper disclosure of generative AI. It imposes disclosure requirements on the use of generative AI tools with customers, and limits a company’s ability to blame gen AI systems. The AI Law [‘expressively prohibits attempting to avoid consumer protection/fraud liability by blaming Gen AI itself as an intervening factor’](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2024/4/utah-enacts-first-ai-focused-consumer-protection-legislation-in-us).

Under the AI Law, companies which violate consumer protection laws cannot defend themselves by claiming that it was the generative AI who caused offence, or disseminated incorrect information. In effect, companies should view the outputs of the generative AI tools they deploy [in a similar manner to statements of their own employees](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2024/04/utah-becomes-first-state).

Accountability is an essential ingredient of responsible AI. Transferring decision-making powers to generative AI systems creates a unique situation in which responsibility and accountability can come apart. However, accountability gaps can, will, and are being filled. Cases such as Utah and Air Canada demonstrate that companies will – in the end – be held accountable for generative AI usage.
