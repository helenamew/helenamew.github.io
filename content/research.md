When we first think of privacy, we might think first of big data; the traces of personal information that scatter as we hover over advertisements and order clothes. We might also think of the power that others have over us in so far as they know information about us. But privacy also matters on a smaller, interpersonal scale. Giving up privacy is one of the main ways in which we build intimacy in intimate relationships, and the extent to which we give (or perhaps are expected to give) information to another person is part of what defines what relationships are.

The idea that intimate relationships are in some essential way a matter of reducing privacy is a widely-accepted judgement and conceptual relation. But if we think of an intimate relationship as one in which privacy is reduced, then we run into various problems and exceptions.

Privacy loss can make us vulnerable, but under some conditions, in certain circumstances, with certain persons, privacy losses facilitate intimacy. How can the vulnerability that makes privacy so important to protect also be valuable to initiate? And under what circumstances does this vulnerability lead to or partially constitute intimacy?

We need a way to explain this dichotomy which both accommodates the idea that privacy is valuable because it can protect us from being known and the idea that privacy can make possible valuable ways of becoming known. Rather than defining intimacy in terms of intimate zones (Gunkel, 2024) or the motivational context in which we interact with others (Inness, 1994) I propose that we should understand intimacy in terms of intimate interactions.

In future work, I hope to apply my doctoral research to think about the future of intimacy. I am especially interested in artificial modes of intimacy and the impact that interactions with AI companions might have both on ourselves and our relationships with others.
